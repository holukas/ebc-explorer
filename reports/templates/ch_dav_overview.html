Where does half the energy go?

At the Davos spruce forest, the measured turbulent fluxes H + LE add up to only about half of the available energy: every year since 1998, with every instrument generation. This page compares CH-Dav with the 84 ICOS and NEON sites of Nicolini et al. (2026), shows what the 27-year record tells us, and lists the explanations that remain.

Aggregation

Longer sums do not rescue it

For most sites the bulk ratio ΣTE / ΣAE climbs towards one as fluxes are summed over days, months and years, because storage and phase effects cancel. CH-Dav stays below 0.5 at every time scale: the missing energy is systematic, not a timing problem.

CH-Dav · ICOS FLUXNET product 1997–2024 · measured values only

27 years, six instrument generations, one gap

The closure slope has stayed between 0.38 and 0.58 since 1998. It did not jump with any change of sonic anemometer or gas analyser, so the cause lies beyond a single instrument. The shift in the energy balance ratio around 2014–2015 coincides with the new sonic and the new soil heat flux plates.

Half the energy is missing at every hour

If heat were only stored in the trees, the gap would peak in the morning and reverse in the evening. Instead the residual follows available energy through the day and peaks at noon. Biomass storage and photosynthesis (green) are real but small: they raise the slope from 0.50 to 0.54.

CH-Dav · daytime · 1998–2024

The gap follows the wind

Almost two thirds of daytime air arrives from the north, along the valley. Exactly this flow closes worst. At the same friction velocity, northerly winds close at about 0.5, while southerly and westerly winds reach 0.75–1.0. The contrast holds in all six instrument periods and in clearly unstable conditions.

    Interpretation

    Possible explanations, weighed

    Ordered from most to least likely to explain the bulk of the gap at CH-Dav, based on the analyses above and the EBC literature. The verdicts are working assessments, not conclusions.

    Terrain-driven advection in the along-valley flow

    Leading candidate
    could explain: most of the north-sector deficit
    • At equal u*, northerly winds close at ~0.5, S and W at 0.75–1.0, in every instrument period since 1998 and for unstable conditions only.
    • North is the dominant daytime direction (62 % of data) and the fastest flow, so it dominates the site average.
    • CH-Dav is the steepest ICOS site in the network (16° by DEM). A beech slope forest shows the same pattern: 0.48–0.55 in the poor sectors vs 0.80, attributed to advection.
    • Horizontal and vertical advection are not measured by a single tower and would bypass the eddy covariance system.

    McGloin et al. 2018; Leuning et al. 2012; Foken 2008

    Secondary circulations and low-frequency transport

    Likely contributor
    flat-terrain models: ~8–19 % of H
    • Closure rises from 0.2 to 0.8 with u*, the classic signature of energy carried by organised motions that 30-min averages miss.
    • The LES-based model of Mauder et al. (2021) predicts about 8 % extra H for this tower height, at most ~19 %; topography is thought to amplify such circulations, but no slope model exists yet.
    • Over tall forest, 30-min fluxes were still growing at the end of the averaging window in 22 % (H) to 47 % (LE) of periods.

    Mauder et al. 2020, 2021, 2024; Wanner et al. 2022, 2024; Charuchittipan et al. 2014

    Coordinate rotation and sonic set-up

    Open question
    sector-wise planar fit: +0.03–0.05 elsewhere
    • On a 16° slope, streamlines tilt differently by wind sector; a misfit leaks horizontal flux into w'T' and w'q'.
    • The EC geometry is barely documented: the boom was found rotated in 2002, a wrong wind-direction configuration was fixed in 2019, and a transducer was found out of place in 2022.
    • Tilt angles and mean w per sector need EddyPro full output to check.

    McGloin et al. 2018; Leuning et al. 2012; ICOS labelling report 2019

    Energy storage in the forest

    Partial
    measured effect: +0.04 in slope
    • Biomass heat storage and photosynthesis raise the slope from 0.50 to 0.54; literature values for tall spruce forests are 5–10 % of midday Rn.
    • Storage uses air temperature as a proxy for stem temperature, which likely underestimates it. Across the network, imbalance grows with biomass, and CH-Dav (62 kg m⁻²) sits far above that trend.
    • The residual peaks at noon and does not reverse in the evening, which storage cannot produce.

    Nicolini et al. 2026; Lindroth et al. 2010; Moderow et al. 2009

    Tower and boom flow distortion

    Sector-limited
    affects easterly winds (~4 % of data)
    • With the boom pointing west, easterly air passes the 3 m lattice tower first. East sectors indeed close worst (0.26–0.47), matching a documented case with the same geometry.
    • Northerly flow reaches the sonic undisturbed by the tower, so this does not explain the main deficit.

    Kidston et al. 2010; Mauder & Zeeman 2018

    Instrument errors: sonic heads, gas analyser, radiometer

    Unlikely as main cause
    known effects: 3–10 %
    • The gap persists with three sonic models, three gas analysers and two radiometers.
    • Known sonic head effects (transducer shadowing, angle of attack) are 3–10 % and only weakly direction-dependent; humidity-related LE damping appears with the open-path analyser too.
    • Radiation is internally consistent and matches an independent PAR sensor; net radiation corrections change closure by a few percent at most.

    Frank et al. 2013, 2016; Horst et al. 2015; Fratini et al. 2012; Nicolini et al. 2026

    Towards closing the gap

    Tests to run, corrections to try

    Tests discriminate between the explanations; corrections are only justified once the cause is known. In line with Nicolini et al. (2026), any residual should be reported as a bounded uncertainty rather than absorbed by a blanket closure correction.
