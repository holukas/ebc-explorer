Energy balance closure at CH-Dav

At CH-Dav, the measured turbulent heat fluxes (H + LE) account for about half of the available energy. This is the case in every year since 1998 and with every instrument set-up. The most frequent daytime wind crosses the Davoser See and the open valley floor before it reaches the forest and the tower. This report compares CH-Dav with 84 ICOS and NEON sites (Nicolini et al., 2026), summarises the 1997 to 2024 record and lists possible causes.

Temporal aggregation

Closure at longer time scales

At most sites, the EBR approaches 1 when fluxes are summed over days to years, because storage changes and timing differences cancel out. At CH-Dav, the EBR stays between 0.43 and 0.47 at all time scales. The missing energy is therefore systematic.

Closure per year

Daily cycle of the energy balance

If heat storage in the trees caused the gap, the residual would peak in the morning and become negative in the evening. Instead, the residual follows the available energy and peaks at noon. Biomass heat storage and photosynthesis are small: including them increases the slope from 0.50 to 0.54.

Error budget of the available energy

Could the gap come from a single term of the available energy, for example from the soil heat flux, which varies strongly from place to place under a forest? The soil heat flux explains at most about 7 % of the missing energy. Even if all terms were wrong in the same direction at the same time, about half of the missing energy would remain.

What was done

  • Used the five soil plots separately: a heat flux plate at 5 to 6 cm, soil temperature at 2 to 5 cm and soil water content at each plot (ICOS L2 sensor data).
  • Calculated the heat stored in the soil layer above each plate from the temperature change and the soil water content. The bulk density of the topsoil is not measured; a range of 0.5 to 1.2 g cm⁻³ was used.
  • Calculated closure with the mean of the plates, with each plate, and with the highest and lowest plate, on the same 13 766 daytime half-hours.
  • Estimated how large the error of each term could be: the difference between the soil plots, the unknown bulk density, air storage from the profile or a single point, biomass storage twice as large, the difference between two pyranometers and the net radiation corrections of Nicolini et al. (2026).

CH-Dav surroundings, swisstopo terrain, surface and landscape models

Terrain, forest and lake around the tower

The tower stands at the foot of a forested slope (15 to 18° within 500 m, facing west-northwest), about 80 m above the flat valley floor. The Davoser See lies 222 m northwest of the tower. For the most frequent daytime wind from the north, the air crosses the lake and the open valley floor, and then only about 250 m of forest, before it reaches the tower.

What was done

  • Downloaded the swisstopo terrain model swissALTI3D (2 m resolution, 8 × 8 km) and surface model swissSURFACE3D (0.5 m, 3 × 4 km) around the tower.
  • Calculated the canopy height as surface minus terrain. Ground with a canopy lower than 2 m counts as open (lake, meadow, road, clearing).
  • Took the outline of the Davoser See from the swisstopo landscape model swissTLM3D.
  • For each 30° wind sector, calculated the terrain profile, the mean canopy height, the share of open ground and the share of lake area, and compared them with the closure (EBR) of that sector.

    Interpretation

    Possible causes

    The causes are ordered from most to least likely to explain most of the gap at CH-Dav. The assessment is based on the analyses above and on the literature, and it is provisional.

    Lake, valley floor and along-valley flow north of the tower

    Most likely
    Possible share: most of the deficit for northerly wind
    • At the same u*, the EBR is about 0.5 for northerly wind and 0.75 to 1.0 for southerly and westerly wind. This holds in all instrument periods since 1998 and in unstable conditions. Northerly wind brings 41 % of the daytime data and dominates the site average.
    • Northerly air crosses the Davoser See (lake covers 49 % of the sector within 500 m and 94 % between 500 and 1000 m) and only about 250 m of forest. The lake stores much of the absorbed radiation in the water and releases little heat during the day.
    • According to the ICOS footprint model, 90 % of the daytime flux comes from within about 190 m, so the lake contributes only about 3 % to the measured flux. Within the northern sector, closure does not change with this share. The lake therefore acts indirectly, not through the footprint.
    • Cool lake air flowing over the warmer forest takes up forest heat below the sensors. This horizontal advection is not measured by a single EC system. The air also rises about 80 m up the slope, which adds vertical advection.
    • At the same u*, closure is poorest near neutral stability, the typical condition of strong along-valley wind, and best in strongly unstable conditions.
    • The NNE sector has little lake but also closes poorly. Both N and NNE blow along the valley axis, so the along-valley flow matters beyond the lake. McGloin et al. (2018) found similar sector-dependent closure at a sloping forest and attributed it to advection.

    McGloin et al. 2018; Leuning et al. 2012; Foken 2008; Chu et al. 2026

    Secondary circulations and slow air motions

    Likely contributor
    Models for flat terrain: 8 to 19 % of H
    • The EBR increases from 0.2 to 0.8 with u*. This is typical for heat carried by large, slow air motions that 30-minute averages do not capture.
    • The model of Mauder et al. (2021) predicts about 8 % additional H for this measurement height, and at most about 19 %. Sloping terrain may increase this effect, but no model for slopes exists yet.
    • The contrast between the cold lake and the warm forest can drive local circulations of the same kind.
    • Above a tall forest, 30-minute fluxes were incomplete in 22 % (H) to 47 % (LE) of the periods (Charuchittipan et al., 2014).

    Mauder et al. 2020, 2021, 2024; Wanner et al. 2022, 2024; Charuchittipan et al. 2014

    Coordinate rotation and sonic set-up

    Unresolved
    Sector-wise planar fit at other sites: +0.03 to +0.05
    • On a 16° slope, the mean wind is tilted differently in each wind sector. If the coordinate rotation does not account for this, part of the horizontal flux is counted in the vertical flux, or the reverse.
    • The set-up of the EC system is poorly documented. The boom was found rotated in 2002, the wind direction configuration was corrected in 2019, and a sonic transducer was found displaced in 2022.
    • Tilt angles and the mean vertical wind per sector can be checked with the EddyPro full output.

    McGloin et al. 2018; Leuning et al. 2012; ICOS labelling report 2019

    Heat storage and soil heat flux

    Partial
    Measured effect: +0.04 in slope
    • The soil heat flux is small under the dense canopy (6 W m⁻² at summer midday, plus 8 W m⁻² stored above the plates). The five soil plots differ by up to 20 W m⁻², which is at most about 7 % of the missing energy.
    • Even with every plausible error of the available-energy terms in the same direction, about half of the missing energy remains (see the error budget).
    • Biomass heat storage and photosynthesis increase the slope from 0.50 to 0.54. Published values for tall spruce forests are 5 to 10 % of Rn at midday.
    • Biomass heat storage is estimated from air temperature, not tree temperature, and is probably underestimated. In the network, the imbalance increases with biomass. CH-Dav (62 kg m⁻²) is well above this relationship.
    • The residual peaks at noon and does not become negative in the evening. Storage cannot cause this pattern.

    Nicolini et al. 2026; Lindroth et al. 2010; Moderow et al. 2009

    Flow distortion by the tower and boom

    Easterly wind only
    Affects about 4 % of the data
    • The boom points west, so easterly wind passes the 3 m wide lattice tower before it reaches the sonic. Easterly sectors have the lowest EBR (0.26 to 0.47). Kidston et al. (2010) found the same at a site with this geometry.
    • Northerly wind reaches the sonic without passing the tower. Flow distortion therefore does not explain the main deficit.

    Kidston et al. 2010; Mauder and Zeeman 2018

    Instrument errors

    Unlikely as main cause
    Known effects: 3 to 10 %
    • The deficit occurs with three sonic models, three gas analysers and two radiometers.
    • Known sonic errors (transducer shadowing, angle of attack) are 3 to 10 % and depend little on wind direction. Humidity-dependent losses of LE also occur with the open-path analyser.
    • The radiation data are consistent and agree with a separate PAR sensor. Corrections of net radiation change closure by a few percent.

    Frank et al. 2013, 2016; Horst et al. 2015; Fratini et al. 2012; Nicolini et al. 2026

    Next steps

    Tests and corrections

    Tests are needed to identify the cause. Corrections are justified only once the cause is known. Following Nicolini et al. (2026), a remaining imbalance should be reported as an uncertainty, not removed with a general closure correction.
