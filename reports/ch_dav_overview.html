Energy balance closure at CH-Dav

At CH-Dav, the measured turbulent heat fluxes (H + LE) account for about half of the available energy. This is the case in every year since 1998 and with every instrument set-up. This report compares CH-Dav with 84 ICOS and NEON sites (Nicolini et al., 2026), summarises the 1997 to 2024 record and lists possible causes.

Temporal aggregation

Closure at longer time scales

At most sites, the EBR approaches 1 when fluxes are summed over days to years, because storage changes and timing differences cancel out. At CH-Dav, the EBR stays between 0.43 and 0.47 at all time scales. The missing energy is therefore systematic.

Closure per year

The yearly closure slope ranged from 0.38 to 0.58 between 1998 and 2024. It did not change when instruments were replaced. The EBR decreased in 2014 and 2015, when the sonic anemometer and the soil heat flux plates were replaced.

Daily cycle of the energy balance

If heat storage in the trees caused the gap, the residual would peak in the morning and become negative in the evening. Instead, the residual follows the available energy and peaks at noon. Biomass heat storage and photosynthesis are small: including them increases the slope from 0.50 to 0.54.

Closure by wind direction

62 % of the daytime data come from north to north-northeast, along the valley. These wind directions have the lowest closure. At the same u*, the EBR is about 0.5 for northerly wind and 0.75 to 1.0 for southerly and westerly wind. This difference occurs in all six instrument periods and also in unstable conditions.

    Interpretation

    Possible causes

    The causes are ordered from most to least likely to explain most of the gap at CH-Dav. The assessment is based on the analyses above and on the literature, and it is provisional.

    Advection in the along-valley flow over sloping terrain

    Most likely
    Possible share: most of the deficit for northerly wind
    • At the same u*, the EBR is about 0.5 for northerly wind and 0.75 to 1.0 for southerly and westerly wind. This holds in all instrument periods since 1998 and in unstable conditions.
    • Northerly wind is the most frequent daytime wind (62 % of the data) and has the highest wind speeds. It therefore dominates the site average.
    • With a slope of 16°, CH-Dav is the steepest ICOS site in the dataset. McGloin et al. (2018) found a similar pattern at a sloping beech forest (EBR 0.48 to 0.55 in some wind sectors, 0.80 in others) and attributed it to advection.
    • Advection is heat carried horizontally or vertically past the tower. A single EC system does not measure it.

    McGloin et al. 2018; Leuning et al. 2012; Foken 2008

    Secondary circulations and slow air motions

    Likely contributor
    Models for flat terrain: 8 to 19 % of H
    • The EBR increases from 0.2 to 0.8 with u*. This is typical for heat carried by large, slow air motions that 30-minute averages do not capture.
    • The model of Mauder et al. (2021) predicts about 8 % additional H for this measurement height, and at most about 19 %. Sloping terrain may increase this effect, but no model for slopes exists yet.
    • Above a tall forest, 30-minute fluxes were incomplete in 22 % (H) to 47 % (LE) of the periods (Charuchittipan et al., 2014).

    Mauder et al. 2020, 2021, 2024; Wanner et al. 2022, 2024; Charuchittipan et al. 2014

    Coordinate rotation and sonic set-up

    Unresolved
    Sector-wise planar fit at other sites: +0.03 to +0.05
    • On a 16° slope, the mean wind is tilted differently in each wind sector. If the coordinate rotation does not account for this, part of the horizontal flux is counted in the vertical flux, or the reverse.
    • The set-up of the EC system is poorly documented. The boom was found rotated in 2002, the wind direction configuration was corrected in 2019, and a sonic transducer was found displaced in 2022.
    • Tilt angles and the mean vertical wind per sector can be checked with the EddyPro full output.

    McGloin et al. 2018; Leuning et al. 2012; ICOS labelling report 2019

    Heat storage in the forest

    Partial
    Measured effect: +0.04 in slope
    • Biomass heat storage and photosynthesis increase the slope from 0.50 to 0.54. Published values for tall spruce forests are 5 to 10 % of Rn at midday.
    • Biomass heat storage is estimated from air temperature, not tree temperature, and is probably underestimated. In the network, the imbalance increases with biomass. CH-Dav (62 kg m⁻²) is well above this relationship.
    • The residual peaks at noon and does not become negative in the evening. Storage cannot cause this pattern.

    Nicolini et al. 2026; Lindroth et al. 2010; Moderow et al. 2009

    Flow distortion by the tower and boom

    Easterly wind only
    Affects about 4 % of the data
    • The boom points west, so easterly wind passes the 3 m wide lattice tower before it reaches the sonic. Easterly sectors have the lowest EBR (0.26 to 0.47). Kidston et al. (2010) found the same at a site with this geometry.
    • Northerly wind reaches the sonic without passing the tower. Flow distortion therefore does not explain the main deficit.

    Kidston et al. 2010; Mauder and Zeeman 2018

    Instrument errors

    Unlikely as main cause
    Known effects: 3 to 10 %
    • The deficit occurs with three sonic models, three gas analysers and two radiometers.
    • Known sonic errors (transducer shadowing, angle of attack) are 3 to 10 % and depend little on wind direction. Humidity-dependent losses of LE also occur with the open-path analyser.
    • The radiation data are consistent and agree with a separate PAR sensor. Corrections of net radiation change closure by a few percent.

    Frank et al. 2013, 2016; Horst et al. 2015; Fratini et al. 2012; Nicolini et al. 2026

    Next steps

    Tests and corrections

    Tests are needed to identify the cause. Corrections are justified only once the cause is known. Following Nicolini et al. (2026), a remaining imbalance should be reported as an uncertainty, not removed with a general closure correction.
